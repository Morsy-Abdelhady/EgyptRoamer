Medinet Habu is the mortuary temple of Ramesses III, on the West Bank of Luxor. Egypt's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities calls it an architectural masterpiece, for its beauty and its good state of preservation. Many visitors combine it with the Valley of the Kings. This guide explains what you will see.

Ramesses III and his temple

King Ramesses III (about 1184–1153 BC) is the most famous king of the Twentieth Dynasty. He fought many wars against the Libyans and the Sea Peoples, which brought years of prosperity to Egypt. At the end of his reign he fell victim to a conspiracy in the royal harem.

He built Medinet Habu as his mortuary temple, for his funeral rituals and for the worship of the god Amun. It follows the usual temple plan of its time and is largely inspired by the Ramesseum.

The temple is part of Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1979.

The walls and the High Gate

A huge mud-brick wall with two guard rooms surrounds the temple. The people living around it used the enclosure to protect themselves and the temple from attacks by Libyans from the west, a sign of the political situation in Ramesses III's reign.

In the middle of the eastern wall stands a great gate between two towers with terraces, known as the Gate of Ramesses III or the High Gate. A similar gate on the western side has been demolished. In ancient times a canal connected to the Nile, with a dock in front, let boats reach the temple.

Inside the temple

From the High Gate, the visit follows the temple's axis:

  1. The first pylon and the first courtyard.
  2. The second pylon and the second courtyard.
  3. Three halls of columns and a group of chapels.
  4. The sanctuary, the Holy of Holies, with a group of rooms behind it.

Look at the columns: some are round with papyrus-shaped capitals, and others show Ramesses III in the form of Osiris. Around the temple are the remains of a royal palace, storehouses, the temple's administration buildings and royal stables.

The reliefs

The walls show Ramesses III in military scenes, such as the Libyan campaigns and the battles against the Sea Peoples. There are also scenes of hunting and of expeditions, and religious scenes: the king worshipping the gods, and various celebrations.

Around the temple

Near the temple stands a temple built by Queen Hatshepsut and King Thutmose III, on the site of a Twelfth Dynasty temple that was built on the tombs of the ancestors of the god Amun. Close by are the tombs of the Divine Wives of Amun-Ra, from the Twenty-fifth and Twenty-sixth Dynasties.

Frequently asked questions

Who built Medinet Habu?

King Ramesses III (about 1184–1153 BC) of the Twentieth Dynasty, as his mortuary temple.

What is Medinet Habu famous for?

Its good state of preservation, its High Gate, its Osiride columns and its reliefs of Ramesses III's wars against the Libyans and the Sea Peoples.

Is Medinet Habu worth visiting?

The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities calls it an architectural masterpiece for its beauty and good preservation. It is on the West Bank, so it fits easily into a day with the Valley of the Kings.

Where is Medinet Habu?

On the West Bank of Luxor, part of Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1979.
