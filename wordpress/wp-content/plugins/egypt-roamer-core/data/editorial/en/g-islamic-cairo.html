In the middle of modern Cairo lies one of the world's oldest Islamic cities, with its mosques, madrasas, gates and markets. UNESCO inscribed Historic Cairo on the World Heritage List in 1979. People live, pray and trade here, so it is a city to walk, not an open-air museum. This guide gives you a route and the landmarks to look for.

A short history

Cairo was founded in 969 as the seat of the Fatimid caliphs, inside walls whose gates still stand: Bab Zuwayla to the south, Bab al-Futuh and Bab al-Nasr to the north. The Ayyubids built the Citadel as the seat of government, and under the Mamluks (1250–1517) the city grew into the largest and most complex Islamic city of its time, the capital of a vast empire. The Ottomans kept its buildings and streets and added their own. Because modern Cairo was built elsewhere in the 19th century, whole historic neighbourhoods survived.

Al-Muizz Street, from gate to gate

Al-Muizz Street, named after the Fatimid caliph al-Mu'izz li-Din Allah, runs about one kilometre through the old city, from Bab al-Futuh in the north to Bab Zuwayla in the south. The government restored its buildings and paving from 1997. Walking north to south, you pass:

  • The Mosque of al-Hakim (1013).
  • Bayt al-Suhaymi, a merchant's house built and extended between 1648 and 1796.
  • The Mosque of al-Aqmar (1125), one of the Fatimid mosques UNESCO singles out.
  • Bayn al-Qasrayn, "between the two palaces", where the Fatimid palaces stood, now lined with some of the city's greatest monuments, among them the complex of Sultan Qalawun (1285).
  • The al-Ghuri complex (1505), south of Al-Azhar Street, near the end of the walk at Bab Zuwayla.

Khan el-Khalili

The bazaar takes its name from a caravanserai built in the 1380s by Jaharkas al-Khalili, master of the stables of the Mamluk sultan Barquq. Today the name covers the whole market district, between Al-Muizz Street and the Al-Hussein Mosque. Its best-known coffeehouse, El-Fishawy, was established in 1773.

Al-Azhar

UNESCO notes that Al-Azhar has been a leading centre of religious learning for the whole Islamic world since its foundation in 970. UNESCO lists its mosque among the significant Fatimid mosques of the old city, with those of al-Hakim and al-Aqmar.

Sultan Hassan and the Citadel

South of the old walls, two great sights face each other:

  • The mosque-madrasa of Sultan Hassan, built in 1356–1363 under the Mamluk sultan an-Nasir Hasan, taught the four Sunni schools of law. Beside it stands the Al-Rifa'i Mosque, built between 1869 and 1912 in a neo-Mamluk style.
  • The Citadel, on a spur of the Mokattam hills, was begun by Saladin around 1176–1183 and was the seat of Egypt's government until 1874. It dominates the city's skyline. Inside are the Mosque of Muhammad Ali (1830–1848) and the Mosque of al-Nasir Muhammad (1318).

Not far away, the Mosque of Ibn Tulun (876–879), older than Cairo itself, is known for its spiral minaret and its vast courtyard.

Planning your visit

  • Dress modestly for the mosques: shoulders and knees covered, and a scarf for women. You take your shoes off at the door.
  • Prayer times: visitors are welcome outside the prayers. Friday midday is the busiest moment.
  • On foot: Al-Muizz Street is best walked from north to south; take a car or taxi between the old city and the Citadel.
  • In the evening: the old city is lively after dark. Our Cairo food tour by night and our guide to Cairo at night show you how to enjoy it.

Frequently asked questions

Is Islamic Cairo a World Heritage site?

Yes. UNESCO inscribed Historic Cairo on the World Heritage List in 1979.

How long is Al-Muizz Street?

About one kilometre, from Bab al-Futuh in the north to Bab Zuwayla in the south.

Can non-Muslims visit the mosques?

Visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times, dressed modestly and with shoes removed. Follow the guardians' instructions at each mosque.
