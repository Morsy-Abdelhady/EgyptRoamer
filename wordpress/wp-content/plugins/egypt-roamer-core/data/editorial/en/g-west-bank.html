Ancient Thebes buried its kings on the West Bank of the Nile, and built their funerary temples there too. Most visitors come for the Valley of the Kings, but the West Bank holds much more: temples, queens' tombs and the village of the craftsmen who cut the royal tombs. This guide covers what else to see.

Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari

Deir el-Bahari is a bay in the cliffs on the West Bank, east of the Valley of the Kings. Its name is Arabic for "northern monastery", after a monastery built there in the 7th century. Three temples stand on the site:

  • The terraced temple of Queen Hatshepsut, built around 1470 BC. Set beneath the cliffs, it is a famous example of architecture that makes the most of its site. It was uncovered in the 1890s beneath the monastery ruins and later partly restored.
  • The funerary temple of King Mentuhotep II, around 1970 BC, which has lost much of its upper structure.
  • A temple of Thutmose III, found by a Polish mission that began restoring Hatshepsut's third terrace in 1968.

The Colossi of Memnon

Two huge quartzite statues of Amenhotep III once flanked the gateway of his funerary temple. The temple is gone, and the statues now sit in the middle of the fields. The name Memnon is a later Greek one.

The Ramesseum

The funerary temple of Ramesses II, dedicated to the god Amun and to the dead king. It was famous for a seated statue of Ramesses about 17 metres high, of which only fragments are left. The shattered colossus inspired Shelley's 1818 poem "Ozymandias". About half of the temple survives, with reliefs of the Battle of Kadesh, the Syrian wars and the Festival of Min.

Medinet Habu

The funerary temple of Ramesses III, also dedicated to Amun, stands within a fortified enclosure entered through towers modelled on Syrian fortresses. Its walls show religious scenes and Ramesses III's wars against the Libyans, the Nubians and the Sea Peoples. A royal palace stood beside the forecourt. In the first millennium AD, a Coptic town called Djeme grew up inside the walls.

At Medinet Habu

Look for Ramesses III's battles among the religious scenes, and take time at the fortified gate.

The Valley of the Queens

A gorge in the hills where queens and some royal children of the 19th and 20th dynasties were buried. More than 90 tombs are known, most with an entrance passage, a few short halls and a sarcophagus chamber. The most famous is that of Nefertari, the favourite queen of Ramesses II. The princes Khaemwaset and Amenherkhepeshef were also buried here.

Deir el-Medina

The village of the craftsmen who worked on the royal tombs, especially in the Valley of the Kings. It is the best-preserved village of its kind. Thousands of inscribed papyri and pottery fragments found here tell scholars about the workers' daily lives. The artisans' own tombs and three temples built for the workers are on the site.

Planning the day

  • Start early: the West Bank is hot and exposed, with little shade.
  • Choose: few people can see everything in one day. Combine the Valley of the Kings with Hatshepsut's temple, then add one or two of the others.
  • Tickets: many sites, and some tombs, have their own ticket. Check on the day which are open.

Frequently asked questions

Is the West Bank part of a World Heritage site?

Yes. The Valley of the Kings, the Valley of the Queens and the other sites of Thebes were added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979.

Can I see Nefertari's tomb?

Its opening and ticket change from time to time. Check on the day.

Hatshepsut's temple or Medinet Habu, if I only have time for one?

Hatshepsut's temple for its setting under the cliffs. Medinet Habu for its war reliefs and fortified gate.
